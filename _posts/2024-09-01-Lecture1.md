This page summarizes the main content of the first lecture and can be used as a recap.<br>

#### 1. The Power EXhaust (PEX) problem

In a Nuclear Fusion Reactor, a significant amount of power crosses the last closed flux surface (LCFS), i.e., the separatrix, and enters the scrape-off layer (SOL). From a simple physics model, it can be treat as the power enters at the upstream, e.g. Outer Midplane, of the SOL, then transport the along magnetic field lines and finnaly arrive at the divertor. The resulting heat flux at the targets, if left unmitigated, can reach values of order of tens of $MW m^{-2}$. This value far exceeds the current enginnering limitaion ~10-20 $MW m^{-2}$ leading to what is known as the Power EXhaust (PEX) problem. Without solving PEW problem, it is impossible to operate the nuclear fusion rators. Over the past thirty years, considerable efforts have been made through both experiments and modellings try to address this challenge. In this workshop we take a modelling viewpoint: we start from basic concept/process, e.g. heating and core balance, fuelling and impurity seeding, and magnetic geometry, and follow how they affect SOL transport and target heat loads.

#### 2. Experiments as a backgroud

On the experimental side, what matters for PEX includes: <br>
1) The power into the SOL $P_{SOL}$, which is the net power crossing the separatrix and is determined by heating and radiation. The heat power can be provided by Ohmic heating, NBI heating, ECRH and ICRH heating.<br>
2) During the operation of the fusion reactor, the fuel D and T are injected by the gas puffing system to sustain the DT reaction. To solve the PEX problem, impurities such as neon or argon are also injected to increase radiation.<br>
3) Before starting the fusion reactor, a vacuum is required to eliminate all oxygen. In addition, impurities generated during the DT fusion reaction, such as helium, and intentionally injected impurities must be removed from the reactor to maintain the DT reaction.<br>
4) A broad set of diagnostics are necessary to measure the parameters of plasma and neutral particles. For example, Thomson scattering and ECE for density and temperature profiles; Langmuir probes for local plasma density and temperature; infrared thermography and thermocouples for target heat flux, and spectroscopy/bolometry for impurities and radiation. <br>

#### 3. Magnetic field
In this PEX workshop we focus on magnetic-confinement devices, specifically tokamaks.The magnetic field is the key actor for SOL transport and has two main components: the toroidal field $B_{T}$, generated by the TF coils, and the poloidal field $B_{\theta}$, produced by the plasma current (with the help of PF coils).Together they form helical field lines. In the SOL, along these helices, power and particles stream rapidly from the outer mid-plane (OMP, the “upstream” reference) to the divertor targets. Magnetic geometry controls how upstream power is mapped onto the surface: the connection length, the flux expansion between upstream and target, and the poloidal tilting angle at the target plate determine the wetted area and thus the peak heat flux. This is why geometry (and later, radiation) is a central lever for power exhaust. In this workshop, we assume the tokamak has a toroidal symmetry about the magnetic. However, for the Stellarator e.g. Wendelstein 7-X, the magnetic field is 3D which is much complex.

#### 4. Plasma fluid transport
From fluid perspective, plasma transport in the SOL has two components. Parallel transport carries energy and particles rapidly along the magnetic field; cross-field transport across flux surfaces is slower compared to parallel transport. These two aspects together decided the SOL width. There are three main drivers for the cross-field transport: 1) particle diffusion that the particles always diffuse from high density to the low density; 2) The anomulous tranport which is believed due to the turbulence; 3) the drifts effectt, e.g. the ExB and diamagnetic drifts.<br>
